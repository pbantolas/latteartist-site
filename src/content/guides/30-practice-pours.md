I poured 30 lattes in a focused practice series, one or two a day, changing one variable at a time and saving a photo and a note for every pour. This is what happened, including the stretch where nothing improved.

Thirty pours is an editorial format, not a promise. I'm reporting what my notes showed.

## The setup

- **Machine and grinder:** my usual home setup, unchanged throughout.
- **Cup:** the same 200 ml rounded ceramic cup for every pour.
- **Milk:** whole milk, same brand, measured to the same level in a 350 ml pitcher.
- **Focus:** centred, well-sized hearts; my pours were consistently small and drifted left.
- **Logging:** a photo from above, a 1–5 rating, and one sentence of notes per pour, saved in LatteArtist immediately after each attempt.

Keeping the equipment constant was the point: with the cup and milk fixed, any change in the photos came from me.

## Pours 1–10: confirming the problem

<!-- PHOTO SLOT: grid of a few representative pours 1–10 with their actual notes as captions. -->

The first ten pours documented the baseline. Two things stood out in the saved photos that I had not admitted to myself:

1. **Every design was small.** All ten of them. The notes kept saying "dropped when the cup felt nearly full," which matches the classic [small-design timing problem](/learn/latte-art-design-too-small/).
2. **The drift had a pattern.** Eight of ten hearts leaned left. I had assumed random inconsistency; the photos said systematic.

Without the saved photos I would have told you my problem was "consistency." The photos said two specific, fixable things.

## Pours 11–20: changing one thing

For this block I changed only the drop timing — spout down at half full, before it felt ready. Everything else stayed the same.

<!-- PHOTO SLOT: pours 11–20 representative grid. -->

Pours 11–14 got *worse*. Dropping early with my old gentle flow flooded the base and washed out the contrast. The photos from these four are grey and ugly, and the notes get grumpy. Around pour 15 I increased the flow during the push to match the earlier drop, and the designs immediately grew. Pours 17–20 produced the biggest hearts of the series so far.

Lesson one: Changing one variable sometimes exposes a second one. The early drop only worked once the push became more decisive.

## Pours 21–30: the plateau and the left drift

With size mostly solved, I worked on the drift using the [symmetry checks](/learn/latte-art-symmetry/), aligning the spout with the cup's centre line before every pour.

<!-- PHOTO SLOT: pours 21–30, including the best pour and one regression. -->

Pours 21–26 went nowhere. Centred one day, drifting the next. The notes show I was aligning the spout but then rotating the cup as I levelled it — something I only caught by [filming one pour from the side](/learn/film-latte-art-pours/) around pour 27. The last three pours were the most consistent of the series.

Final tally: of the last ten pours, six were hearts I would happily serve. Of the first ten, one was, by luck.

## What 30 pours taught me

1. **My memory of my pours was wrong.** The saved photos corrected it within the first week.
2. **I got worse before I got better, twice.** Both times the notes showed the regression was a different problem, not a lost skill.
3. **One variable per block worked; one variable per pour did not.** I needed several pours per change before the photos were readable.
4. **The filming detour was worth it.** One side-view video solved what six photo reviews could not.

## Try your own series

Pick one focus (size, symmetry, whatever your photos say) and commit to a block of pours with a photo and one sentence each. The [practice routine](/learn/latte-art-practice-routine/) has the session structure, and LatteArtist keeps the photos, ratings, and notes together so pour 25 can be compared against pour 5 without digging through your camera roll.

For the bigger picture of what progress tends to look like, see the [learning milestones](/learn/how-long-to-learn-latte-art/). Expect your series to look different from mine.
