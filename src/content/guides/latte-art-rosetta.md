The rosetta is the heart with one extra skill: a side-to-side wiggle that lays down a row of leaves before the cut-through.

If yours comes out as a stripe, a blob with stripes, or a fern that erases itself at the end, the fix is usually timing or milk, not wrist speed.

Make sure you can pour a centred [heart](/learn/latte-art-heart/) first. The rosetta reuses the same base, height change, and cut-through.

## 1. Set up exactly like a heart

Swirl the pitcher until the milk looks glossy, like wet paint. Tilt the cup, start high near the centre, and build an even brown base.

Rosettas need more room than hearts, so keep the base modest. If the cup is already half full of base, the leaves will have nowhere to grow.

## 2. Drop low at the back of the cup

When the cup is about half full, bring the spout close to the surface — but start at the side of the cup furthest from you, not the middle.

This is the difference from a heart. The rosetta grows towards you: you will wiggle in one spot for a moment, then drag the pattern backwards.

## 3. Start the wiggle

Increase the flow and begin a small, even side-to-side movement. Think rocking the pitcher, not shaking it.

Each swing lays down a leaf. The leaves stack on top of each other and push the earlier ones forward into a fan.

- Wiggle comes from a loose wrist rocking side to side, with the arm mostly still.
- The movement should be narrow. Wide sweeps smear the leaves instead of stacking them.
- If the leaves look like separate rings instead of a fan, you are moving the pitcher backwards too early.

## 4. Drag backwards slowly

Once the fan forms, start pulling the pitcher towards you while you keep wiggling. Slow and steady.

Dragging too fast stretches the leaves into thin lines with gaps between them. Dragging too slowly piles them into a dense blob with no definition.

The cup should be nearly full as you reach the near edge. If you run out of room halfway, your base was too big or your wiggle started too early.

## 5. Cut through without the crash

Ease the flow, lift slightly, and pull a thin stream through the whole pattern, from the far leaves to the near edge.

The **crash** is the common failure here: finishing with too much flow so the last white dump floods the leaves you just laid. It happens when the cup is full but you keep pouring at wiggle flow rate.

Finish with a thin, lifted stream and stop the pour decisively. A slightly smaller rosetta with clean leaves beats a full cup with a washed-out tail.

## Fix the rosetta you poured

| What showed up | Try this next |
| --- | --- |
| A stripe, not leaves | Start the wiggle before you start dragging |
| A blob with stripes on top | Wiggle narrower and increase flow |
| Thin, gappy leaves | Drag backwards more slowly |
| Dense leaves, no gaps | Drag faster or wiggle wider |
| Pattern erased at the end | Lift and thin the stream before the cut — that was the crash |
| Rosetta only fills half the cup | Make less base and start the wiggle earlier |
| Off-centre leaves | Line the spout up with the middle of the cup, as in the [symmetry guide](/learn/latte-art-symmetry/) |

## A three-pour rosetta session

1. First pour: practise only the wiggle in place. Ignore the drag.
2. Second pour: add the drag and count your leaves out loud to keep the rhythm even.
3. Third pour: keep the rhythm and work on a lifted, thin cut-through.

Note what changed — “wiggle good, dragged too fast, crashed the finish” is enough. The [practice routine](/learn/latte-art-practice-routine/) shows how to turn those notes into a repeatable session.
