Latte art is easier to understand when you stop treating the finished pattern as one complicated move. A pour is a short sequence: prepare fluid microfoam, mix a base, bring the spout close, place white foam on the surface, and finish the design.

For a beginner, the goal is not a perfect rosetta. It is to make each part of that sequence repeatable.

## What you need to begin

You need fresh espresso, milk that suits your diet, a steam wand, a pitcher with a defined spout, and a cup you can tilt comfortably. Keep the pitcher and cup consistent while you learn. If both change every day, it becomes harder to tell whether the milk or your movement caused the result.

Start with the safety and operating guidance for your own espresso machine. Steam systems differ in power, wand angle, and tip design, so a video made on another machine can show the right principle but the wrong timing for yours.

## The four foundations

### 1. Texture milk that can still move

Latte-art milk needs tiny bubbles distributed through the liquid. Baristas call this **microfoam**. The familiar phrase “wet paint” describes how it should look and move: glossy, cohesive, and fluid rather than stiff like spoonable foam.

If the pitcher has hot liquid below and a dry cap of foam above, they will leave the pitcher at different times. You may get brown coffee first and a white blob at the end. Our [milk-steaming guide](/learn/how-to-steam-milk-for-latte-art) breaks the process into adding air and then integrating it.

### 2. Set a clean base

Begin the pour with the pitcher higher above the cup and use a controlled stream. From this height, milk passes below the surface and mixes with the espresso. People often call this the **base** or **canvas**.

Keep the stream directed near the centre and move deliberately. A rushed or uneven base can leave a mottled surface that makes the pattern harder to read.

### 3. Get close enough to draw

White foam appears when the pitcher spout moves close to the surface. Height changes the job of the milk:

- Higher pour: the stream dives below and mixes.
- Lower pour: the textured milk can spread across the surface.

If you stay high for the full pour, the cup may fill without any white design. If you begin very low, you can flood the surface before there is enough base.

### 4. Control flow, then add movement

Flow rate matters before wiggles do. Practise starting and stopping a steady stream. For your first pattern, skip complex side-to-side motion and learn a [simple heart](/learn/latte-art-heart). It teaches cup tilt, pitcher height, placement, flow, and the final **cut-through** in one compact design.

## A beginner learning order

Use this sequence instead of jumping between patterns:

1. Steam milk that stays glossy while you swirl it.
2. Pour an even brown base without trying to draw.
3. Make a white circle by lowering the spout near the surface.
4. Turn the circle into a heart with a controlled cut-through.
5. Repeat the heart until you can identify one change to make next time.
6. Move to a stacked tulip, then later to a rosetta.

That progression is useful because each new pattern keeps earlier skills. A tulip is not an escape from a difficult heart; it asks for the heart’s control several times in one cup.

## What to look at after every pour

Do not judge the result only as “good” or “bad.” Look for one observable symptom:

- **No white appears:** the spout may be too high, the milk may lack texture, or the cup may already be too full.
- **A stiff blob drops at the end:** the foam may have separated or become too thick.
- **The design is tiny:** you may have started drawing late or used too little flow.
- **The design runs to one side:** cup tilt, spout position, or the base may be off-centre.
- **Lines lose definition:** the texture, flow, and distance from the surface are all useful variables to inspect.

Use the [latte-art troubleshooting guide](/learn/latte-art-troubleshooting) to move from the symptom to a small test. One photo rarely proves a single cause, so test rather than guess.

## Make practice deliberate

A useful practice session can be three pours. Keep the same milk, cup, pitcher, and pattern. Choose one focus—such as drawing earlier—and record what you changed. On the next session, keep that change if it helped.
